Points at rational distance from the vertices of the unit square: a whole-plane search to denominator 107 and all vertex-centred circles of height at most 1000

wand125 · November 2026 · draft for preview

Abstract. It is not known whether some point of the plane has rational distances to all four vertices of the unit square. We report two computations that bear on this question; neither settles it. First, an exhaustive search shows that no such point has coordinates with least common denominator at most \(10^7\), anywhere in the plane. A second, simpler search shows the same for denominators up to \(10^{11}\) inside the box \([-9,10]^2\). The first search enumerates integer parameters through necessary arithmetic conditions, generates admissible residue classes by the Chinese remainder theorem, and reconstructs every surviving candidate with exact integer arithmetic. Second, every such point lies on a circle of rational radius \(r\) about a vertex. For every rational \(r \ne 1\) with \(\max(|\text{numerator}|, \text{denominator}) \le 1000\), which is 608,382 circles, we prove that the circle carries no such point; the circle \(r = 1\) is excluded by hand. Hence each of the four distances of a hypothetical point has height greater than 1000. The circle proofs combine a descent through square classes with \(p\)-adic tests, genus-one quotients of rank zero, a refined 2-descent over \(\mathbb{Q}(i)\), and a Mordell–Weil sieve. For the last four circles the sieve needed generators of height 79 to 129, which we obtained from a Heegner point, from 2-coverings of isogenous curves and from 4-coverings. The circle proofs are computer-assisted. They depend on the correctness of PARI/GP and Sage, and one circle uses a rank bound from a single system. We found no earlier work that excludes circles about a vertex one by one, and no verifiable whole-plane search reaching \(10^7\); Section 2.6 states what is and is not new.

1. Introduction and results

Let \(V_1 = (0,0)\), \(V_2 = (1,0)\), \(V_3 = (0,1)\), \(V_4 = (1,1)\). We call a point \(P \in \mathbb{R}^2\) a four-distance point if \(|P - V_j| \in \mathbb{Q}\) for \(j = 1, \dots, 4\). Such a point has rational coordinates, since \(|P-V_2|^2 - |P-V_1|^2 = 1 - 2x\) and similarly for \(y\). Whether a four-distance point exists is an open problem (Guy's Unsolved Problems in Number Theory, problem D19). Points with three rational distances are dense in the plane, and several parametric families of them are known; Section 2 summarises what has been proved and computed.

We write \(P = (X/N, Y/N)\) with integers \(X, Y, N\), \(N \ge 1\) and \(\gcd(X, Y, N) = 1\), and call \(N\) the denominator of \(P\). The height of a rational number \(a/b\) in lowest terms is \(\max(|a|, |b|)\).

Result 1 (finite search).

(a) No four-distance point has denominator \(N \le 10^7\). This covers the whole plane, inside and outside the square.

(b) No four-distance point in the box \([-9, 10]^2\) has denominator \(N \le 10^{11}\).

Result 1 is the outcome of two finite computations (Sections 3.1–3.5 and 3.6). It is not a proof that four-distance points do not exist, and it says nothing about larger denominators.

Result 2 (computer-assisted proof).

Let \(P\) be a four-distance point. Then each of the four distances \(|P - V_j|\) is a rational number of height greater than 1000.

If \(|P - V_j| = r\), the symmetries of the square move \(V_j\) to the origin and keep the distance, so it suffices to show that no circle \(|P| = r\) about the origin with \(r\) of height at most 1000 carries a four-distance point. For \(r = 1\) this is elementary (Section 4.1). For the other 608,382 values of \(r\) we prove it circle by circle (Sections 4 and 5). The proof is computer-assisted; Section 6 lists exactly which software outputs it depends on.

The two results constrain different quantities. A point of denominator \(N\) has distances with denominators dividing \(N\), but their numerators can be large; conversely a distance of small height does not bound \(N\). Neither result implies the other.

Taken together.

Combining Results 1 and 2 with known necessary conditions (Sections 2 and 3.2), a four-distance point \(P = (X/N, Y/N)\) in lowest terms, if one exists, must satisfy all of the following.

  • \(N > 10^7\); and \(N > 10^{11}\) if \(P \in [-9, 10]^2\).
  • \(24 \mid N\). Each distance is \(k/N\) with \(k\) odd and prime to 3, so its reduced denominator is also a multiple of 24.
  • Each distance, written in lowest terms, has numerator or denominator greater than 1000.
  • After swapping the coordinates, \(X\) is odd and \(4 \mid Y\); every prime factor of \(X - Y\) and of \(X + Y - N\) is \(\equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8\).
  • \(P\) is not on a side of the square [6], nor on the lines containing the sides, the diagonals or the midlines [7].

These conditions do not reduce the problem to a finite computation; see Section 2.6.

2. Related work

2.1 Origin and status

The problem is §D19 of Guy's book [1]. The earliest printed source we could trace is C. W. Dodge's Problem 966 in Mathematics Magazine (1976), with a partial solution by G. Shute and K. L. Yocom (1977) [2]. The problem is usually stated for the whole plane [1, 5, 6]; several recent papers restrict to the interior [7, 8, 12, 13, 15]. All recent sources we checked state that it is open [10, 11, 12].

2.2 Three-distance points and the four-distance surface

Points with rational distances to three vertices are dense in the plane; one-parameter families go back to J. H. Hunter (1967) and to Leech, Conway and M. Guy, as recorded by Berry [3]. Berry identified the three-distance surface as a Kummer surface with infinitely many families of rational curves, and showed that the four-distance surface is a double cover of it, branched along two conjugate elliptic quartics over \(\mathbb{Q}(i)\), and of general type with geometric genus 5 [3]. He also showed that his families of degree 2 and 4 contain no four-distance point. This double cover over \(\mathbb{Q}(i)\) is why \(\mathbb{Q}(i)\) appears in Sections 4.4 and 5.1. Corvaja, Turchet and Zannier proved that the distance surfaces for four points in general position are simply connected and of general type [11]. Love studied the three-distance curves on families of lines [10]. Bremner and Ulas found four-distance points for rectangles with a dense set of aspect ratios, never inside the rectangle [5].

Under the Bombieri–Lang conjecture the rational points of a surface of general type are not Zariski dense; Tao [17] and Shaffaf [18] derive from it that no rational distance set is dense in the plane. Solymosi and de Zeeuw proved unconditionally that a rational distance set on an algebraic curve without line or circle components is finite [19]. None of these results decides the unit-square problem.

2.3 Special loci and one-parameter fibrations

Barbara showed that there is no four-distance point on the sides of the square [6]. Ji excluded the diagonals, the midlines and the lines containing the sides, and some lines parallel to a side, and states that the arguments extend to the whole plane [7]. Ieno gave divisibility conditions on the integer model [8]. McCloskey gave a 3-adic condition for rectangles and excluded Berry's family of degree 8 [9].

Two works fix one parameter and test the resulting fibres, which is closest to our Section 4. A MathOverflow question fixes the line \(x = r\), on which points with rational distance to two vertices correspond to points on an elliptic curve, and proposes excluding a line when that curve has rank 0; it reports a few values of \(r\) and no systematic result [14]. Si fixes a signed sum or difference of two distances, so that the fibres are confocal conics, and shows that each fibre lies on a curve of genus 5, gives a rank-zero criterion and analyses one fibre in detail; the work treats interior points and has not been refereed [12]. The congruences \(24 \mid N\) and the conditions modulo 8 that we use in Section 3.2 appear in [8, 12].

2.4 Searches

Pegg collected three-distance points from Heronian triangles [16]. Si searched the interior to common denominator \(10^5\) [13]. A repository by Mulatram-UwU reports a search of the interior to common denominator \(5 \times 10^{10}\), by enumerating Pythagorean partners [15]; it states that it was produced by an AI agent, and we have not checked its code. Our box search (Section 3.6) uses the same idea of enumerating partners, extended to signed coordinates; the code is our own. Forum posts by T. S. Roberts (c. 2013) report larger whole-plane bounds that we could not verify. We found no refereed exhaustive search.

2.5 Methods

The tools of Sections 4 and 5 are standard: 2-descent and Simon's algorithm as implemented in PARI/GP [25, 30]; 4-descent and the minimisation and reduction of coverings [22, 23, 24]; Heegner points [26, 28] and Kolyvagin's theorem [27]; and the Mordell–Weil sieve [20, 21]. The conversion of a quartic with a rational point to Weierstrass form follows [29, Thm 2.17].

2.6 What is new here

Within the literature we surveyed (Sections 2.1–2.5), the following points have no precedent. "No precedent" means that we did not find one; the survey covered journals, arXiv, Zenodo, MathOverflow, Mathematics Stack Exchange and public repositories up to November 2026.

  • We found no earlier work that fixes the distance to one vertex and proves the resulting circles empty one by one. Result 2 does this for all 608,382 circles of height at most 1000, so each distance of a four-distance point has height greater than 1000. The nearest precedents fix a vertical line [14] or a sum or difference of two distances [12]; neither excludes fibres systematically over a range.
  • Among whole-plane searches whose method and output we could check, none reached denominator \(10^7\). Forum posts by Roberts report larger whole-plane bounds that we could not verify.
  • The box \([-9,10]^2\) contains the square, so the box search also covers the interior up to denominator \(10^{11}\). The largest interior search we found reports \(5 \times 10^{10}\) [15]; our box search uses its idea of enumerating partners.

What is not new: the individual methods (descent, Heegner points, coverings, the Mordell–Weil sieve) are standard [20–30]; the congruences of Section 3.2 are known [8, 12]. Both searches are finite computations. Under Bombieri–Lang the four-distance points would lie on finitely many curves, so closing all circles of small height does not reduce the problem to a finite computation; Result 2 removes only circles of height at most 1000.

3. The whole-plane search

3.1 Integer model and normalisation

Scale the square by \(N\). A four-distance point of denominator \(N\) gives integers \(A, B, C, D \ge 0\) with

\[ A^2 = X^2 + Y^2,\quad B^2 = (N - X)^2 + Y^2,\quad C^2 = X^2 + (N - Y)^2,\quad D^2 = (N - X)^2 + (N - Y)^2 . \]

The symmetry group of the square has order 8, so we may assume \(X \le Y \le N/2\). The coordinates are not otherwise bounded: \(X\) may be negative, which is how points outside the square enter. Put \(p = B - A\) and \(q = C - A\). From \(B^2 - A^2 = N^2 - 2NX\) and \(C^2 - A^2 = N^2 - 2NY\) we get \(B \ge C \ge A\), and the triangle inequality gives \(0 \le q \le p \le N\). The coordinates are recovered as

\[ X = \frac{N^2 - p^2 - 2Ap}{2N}, \qquad Y = \frac{N^2 - q^2 - 2Aq}{2N}, \]

and \(A^2 = X^2 + Y^2\) becomes the quadratic \(4QA^2 - 4TA + R = 0\) with

\[ Q = p^2 + q^2 - N^2,\quad T = p(N^2 - p^2) + q(N^2 - q^2),\quad R = (N^2 - p^2)^2 + (N^2 - q^2)^2, \] \[ T^2 - QR = K = (N^2 - p^2)(N^2 - q^2)\bigl(2N^2 - (p - q)^2\bigr). \]

So a triple \((N, p, q)\) determines at most two candidate points, and \(K\) must be a perfect square (the degenerate cases \(Q = 0\) are handled separately). Enumerating all triples would cost about \(N^3/576\) per denominator range, which is too much at \(10^7\); the rest of this section is about avoiding that.

3.2 Necessary conditions

For a primitive four-distance point the following hold. The first group is known (Section 2); we use it, we do not claim it.

  • \(24 \mid N\), the four distances are odd and prime to 3, and after swapping coordinates \(X\) is odd and \(4 \mid Y\).
  • Every prime factor of \(X - Y\) and of \(X + Y - N\) is \(\equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8\). The reason is that 2 is a non-residue modulo a prime \(\ell \equiv \pm 3 \pmod 8\), so \(\ell\) dividing the sum or difference of two legs at a vertex would force \(\ell\) to divide \(N\) and both coordinates.

Put \(n = N/2\), \(u = p/2\), \(v = q/2\) and \(h = u - v\). These are integers, and \(X = n - u(A+u)/n\), \(Y = n - v(A+v)/n\). The search uses the following further conditions. Each is a necessary condition, so no four-distance point is lost; the proofs are in the repository documents cited in Section 7.

  1. Integrality as a generator. A common integer \(A\) making \(X\) and \(Y\) integral exists exactly when \(\gcd\bigl(n/\gcd(n,u),\, n/\gcd(n,v)\bigr) \mid u - v\). For fixed \(h\) this is equivalent to: for every prime power \(\ell^e \parallel n\), with \(t = \ell^{\max(e - v_\ell(h), 0)}\), we have \(u \equiv 0\) or \(u \equiv h \pmod t\). The search combines these alternatives by the Chinese remainder theorem and generates only the admissible \(u\). Pairs that would fail integrality are never formed.
  2. Conditions on the difference \(h\). Since \(X - Y = -h(A + u + v)/n\) is odd with no prime factor \(\equiv \pm 3 \pmod 8\), the part of \(h\) made of such primes divides \(n\), and an even \(h\) has the same 2-adic valuation as \(n\). In addition \(h/n\) must lie in residue classes modulo \(5 \cdot 11 \cdot 19\) found by enumerating the four distance equations over the finite fields (for example \(h/n \equiv 2, 3 \pmod 5\)).
  3. Valuations. If \(\ell = 2\) or \(\ell \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) and \(\ell^e \parallel n\), then \(\ell^{1}, \dots, \ell^{e-1}\) cannot be the exact power of \(\ell\) dividing \(u\) or \(v\); for \(\ell = 2\), \(e \ge 2\), the valuation \(e + 1\) is also impossible.
  4. Common factor. With \(g = \gcd(n, u, v)\), \(n_0 = n/g\) and \(Q_0 = (u/g)^2 + (v/g)^2 - n_0^2\), we need \(g \mid n_0 Q_0\).
  5. Local solubility. The four distance equations must be solvable modulo each prime from 5 to 43. Later versions add conditions modulo \(125, 49, 121, 169\) and at further primes up to 281. The tables come from exhaustive enumeration over the finite rings, checked by a second, independent enumeration. A new condition was adopted only after the parameter sets that survive it agreed with those obtained by applying it after the previous version.

A candidate that passes is reconstructed exactly: \(K\) must be a square, the divisions in the formula for \(A\), \(X\), \(Y\) must be exact, and all four distance identities are checked with exact integers.

Size of the enumeration. For fixed \(n\) and \(h\), with \(m = n/\gcd(n, h)\), at most \(2^{\omega(m)}(1 + n/m)\) values of \(u\) are generated. Summing over \(h\) gives at most \(2n\,\tau(n^2) \le 2n\,d_3(n)\) per \(n\), and \(\sum_{n \le M} d_3(n) \le M H_M^2\). The number of generated pairs up to denominator \(B\) is therefore \(O(B^2 \log^2 B)\), against \(\Theta(B^3)\) triples \((N, p, q)\).

3.3 Exact arithmetic

At \(N = 10^7\) the quantity \(K\) reaches \(2N^6\) (141 bits), and \(X^2 + Y^2\) and \(D^2\) reach \(2(N^3 + N)^2\) (141 bits). Earlier versions of the program used 128-bit integers and were therefore restricted to \(N \le 2 \times 10^6\). The version used beyond that bound (v5) computes these quantities with 256-bit unsigned integers, tests squares by residues modulo 64, 63, 65 and 11 followed by an exact bitwise integer square root, and never uses floating point in a decision. It follows the frozen earlier version step for step: same formulas, same order of roots, same counters. Only the width of the integers differs.

3.4 Validation

  • For \(N \le 12\), with the four-distance conditions removed, the reconstruction returns exactly the integer three-distance points found by brute force over all coordinates allowed by a proven radius bound, including points outside the square.
  • The 256-bit routines were checked on about 720,000 squares \(r^2, r^2 - 1, r^2 + 2r\) and against Python big integers.
  • Three-distance points with \(N \le 12\) were scaled by factors up to \(N \approx 10^7\); every scaled point was recovered.
  • Each new version (v5 in C++; v6, v7, v8 in Rust) was accepted only if, on common ranges up to \(N = 2 \times 10^6\), it produced the same set of parameter triples reaching reconstruction, the same three-distance witnesses and the same counters as its predecessor. v6 is a port of v5 written with the help of OpenAI Codex and accepted by this comparison.
  • As a positive control, every run lists the three-distance points that pass all filters ("witnesses"). These are re-checked with Python integers.

3.6 A partner search in a bounded box

A second program checks a bounded region by a different route. Write \(P = (A/Q, C/Q)\) with \(B = Q - A\), \(D = Q - C\); then \(P\) is a four-distance point exactly when \(A^2 + C^2\), \(B^2 + C^2\), \(A^2 + D^2\) and \(B^2 + D^2\) are all squares. With \(M = 10N\), the program examines every \((Q, A, C)\) with \(Q \le N\) and \(\max(|A|, |B|, |C|, |D|) \le M\), which covers every point of \([-9, 10]^2\) with denominator at most \(N\). For each \(c = |C| \le M\) it lists the partners \(v \le M\) with \(c^2 + v^2\) a square, from the factorisations \(c^2 = uw\); each pair of partners \(a < b\) gives \(Q = a + b\) or \(Q = b - a\), and \(d = |Q - c|\) or \(Q + c\) is tested. The same congruences as in Section 3.2 (\(12 \mid Q\), parities modulo 4, divisibility by 3) prune the pairs. The idea of enumerating partners is taken from [15], extended to signed coordinates; the program, in Rust, is our own.

Validation: with the pruning switched off, the three-distance rows agree exactly with an independent brute force on five boxes up to \((N, M) = (720, 2160)\); with it switched on they agree with the brute force after the same conditions are applied; the interior counts of partners and pairs agree with the counts published in [15] for \(N = 10^4, \dots, 10^7\); and each faster version reproduced all counters and rows of its predecessor on common ranges. Squares are tested exactly in integers.

4. Circles about a vertex

4.1 Parametrisation

Fix a rational \(r > 0\) and parametrise the circle \(|P| = r\) by

\[ P(t) = \left( \frac{r(1 - t^2)}{1 + t^2}, \frac{2rt}{1 + t^2} \right), \qquad t \in \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q}), \]

with \(t = \infty\) giving \((-r, 0)\). Then \(|P(t) - V_j|^2 = N_j(t)/(1 + t^2)\) for \(j = 2, 3, 4\), where

\[ N_2 = (r-1)^2 + (r+1)^2 t^2,\quad N_3 = (r^2+1)(1+t^2) - 4rt,\quad N_4 = (r^2+2)(1+t^2) - 2r(1-t^2) - 4rt . \]

A rational \(t\) gives a four-distance point exactly when \((1+t^2)N_j(t)\) is a square for \(j = 2, 3, 4\). The four quadratics \(f_1 = 1 + t^2, f_2 = N_2, f_3 = N_3, f_4 = N_4\) are positive definite with roots in \(\mathbb{Q}(i)\). The curve defined by these three conditions has genus 9. Among its quotients are six curves of genus one, \(v^2 = f_a f_b\) for the six pairs \(\{a, b\}\); every four-distance point gives a rational point on each of them with the same \(t\).

For \(r = 1\) we have \(N_2 = 4t^2\) and \(N_3 = 2(t-1)^2\). For \(t \notin \{0, 1, \infty\}\) both \(1 + t^2\) and \(2(1 + t^2)\) would have to be squares, which is impossible. The values \(t = 0, 1, \infty\) give \((1,0)\), \((0,1)\) and \((-1,0)\), each at distance \(\sqrt 2\) from some vertex.

4.2 Descent through a common square class

At a four-distance point \(t = a/b\), the four values \(F_j(a, b)\) (the binary forms of the \(f_j\)) lie in one square class \(d \cdot \mathbb{Q}^{\times 2}\), where \(d > 0\) is squarefree and divisible only by primes in the set \(S\) of primes dividing the pairwise resultants. For every such \(d\) we look for a prime \(p \le 2000\) such that the four values \(d F_j\) are never simultaneously squares in \(\mathbb{Q}_p\) for \(x \in \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q}_p)\). If every \(d\) fails at some prime, the circle has no four-distance point.

The \(p\)-adic test splits \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Z}_p)\) into discs and declares "no point" on a disc only when the square class of every value is determined on it. Undetermined discs count as possibly containing a point, so only "no point" verdicts are used, and they are proofs. Because the verdict depends only on the class of \(d\) in \(\mathbb{Q}_p^\times / \mathbb{Q}_p^{\times 2}\), the search over \(d\) becomes a system of linear equations over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) in the exponent vector of \(d\), solved by branching.

This step excludes 606,491 of the 608,382 circles. Every circle that survives it has \(12 \mid \operatorname{denominator}(r)\).

4.3 Genus-one quotients of rank zero

If one of the six quotients has Mordell–Weil rank 0, its rational points are its torsion points. When PARI/GP's ellrank returns upper bound 0 and the points found (including points at infinity) account for the whole torsion group, the list of rational \(t\) is complete, and we check each \(t\) exactly. This excludes 588 circles. Seven more circles have a quotient whose rank we prove to be 0 from \(L(E, 1) \ne 0\) with an explicit error bound and Kolyvagin's theorem.

4.4 Refined 2-descent over \(\mathbb{Q}(i)\)

Write \(f_j = c_j (t - \alpha_j)(t - \bar\alpha_j)\) with \(\alpha_j \in \mathbb{Q}(i)\). At a rational point \(t = a/b\) the class of \(t - \alpha_j\) has the form \(b \cdot \delta_j\) with \(\delta_j\) in the 2-Selmer-type group \(\mathbb{Q}(i)(S, 2)\) and \(b \in \mathbb{Q}^\times\). The unknowns are the exponent vectors of the \(\delta_j\) and, for each prime, the local class of \(b\). The constraints are a global one, \(c_j N(\delta_j) \equiv c_1 N(\delta_1)\) in \(\mathbb{Q}^\times / \mathbb{Q}^{\times 2}\), and local ones at each prime of \(S\) up to \(10^6\), expressed through Hilbert symbols. Discs where the local image is not determined are treated as allowing every class, so the local images are over-approximated and "no solution" is a proof. We ran it on pairs of factors and, for some circles, on all four factors at once. It excludes 1,171 circles in total.

A first implementation contained an error: a local stage with no remaining constraint was returned with the same marker as a contradiction, because [] == 0 is true in GP. This made local images too small. It was found by a positive control (four circles carrying real points with three rational distances were wrongly excluded). All affected verdicts were discarded and recomputed with the corrected code, which was then tested on 2,579 cases of circles carrying real points, with primes up to 400 and up to \(10^6\), in two PARI versions. No real point was lost.

4.5 Mordell–Weil sieve

For the remaining circles, choose a quotient \(C: v^2 = f_a f_b\) with a known rational point and map it to a Weierstrass model \(E\) (Washington, Elliptic Curves, Theorem 2.17). A four-distance point gives \(P = \sum n_i G_i + T \in E(\mathbb{Q})\) with generators \(G_i\) and torsion \(T\). For an integer \(M\) we track the classes \((n \bmod M, T)\). At each prime \(\ell\) such that the orders of all \(G_i\) in \(E(\mathbb{F}_\ell)\) divide \(M\), we compute the point of each class modulo \(\ell\), map it back to \(t \bmod \ell\), and remove the class if one of the two remaining products \(f_a f_c\) is a non-zero non-square modulo \(\ell\). Undecidable cases (points with \(v = 0\), \(\ell\) dividing a denominator) keep the class. If every class is removed, the circle has no four-distance point.

Generators come from ellrank (lower bound equal to upper bound) and are saturated. Each exclusion was checked in three ways: (i) positive controls, where the real conditions are replaced by random quartics that are squares at a known rational point, so a correct sieve must keep that class (no control was wrongly removed); (ii) a direct cross-check of small multiples \(\sum n_i G_i + T\) against the class tests (no violation); (iii) an independent second system in Sage with eclib, which checks the \(j\)-invariant against the invariants of the quartic, torsion, saturation index 1 with the height bound, and the rank. The rank in (iii) comes from mwrank; where mwrank could not decide, it comes from the root number and a rigorously bounded \(L(E,1)\) or \(L'(E,1) \ne 0\), via Gross–Zagier and Kolyvagin. The sieve excluded 116 circles that no earlier step had excluded.

5. The last nine circles

After Section 4 nine circles remained: \(r = 607/120, 863/360, 967/360, 167/480, 727/720, 343/960, 887/960, 911/960, 383/984\). On each, a search for rational \(t\) of height up to \(3 \times 10^6\) on a suitable quotient found no four-distance point. They were closed as follows.

5.1 Rank zero over \(\mathbb{Q}(i)\) (five circles)

Every \(f_j\) has a root \(t_0 \in \mathbb{Q}(i)\), so the quotient \(C\) of pair \(\{1, 4\}\) is isomorphic over \(K = \mathbb{Q}(i)\) to an elliptic curve \(E\), with \(t_0\) sent to the origin. Let \(J\) be the Jacobian of \(C\) over \(\mathbb{Q}\) and \(J^{(-1)}\) its quadratic twist by \(-1\).

  • If \(J\) and \(J^{(-1)}\) both have rank 0, then \(E(K)\) is finite. We list \(E(K)_{\mathrm{tors}}\), map each point back to \(C\), and keep the rational \(t\). (607/120, 863/360, 967/360.)
  • If only \(J(\mathbb{Q})\) has rank 0, complex conjugation acts on \(E(K)\) as \(\sigma(Q) = \tau(Q) + c\), with \(\tau\) a group automorphism of order dividing 2 and \(c = \sigma(O)\). A point of \(C(\mathbb{Q})\) gives \(Q\) with \(\sigma(Q) = Q\), so \(S = Q + \tau(Q)\) is fixed by \(\tau\) and \(2Q = S + c\). The \(\tau\)-fixed subgroup is isomorphic to \(J(\mathbb{Q})\), hence finite. We list all \(Q\) with \(2Q = S + c\) for \(\tau\)-fixed torsion \(S\), keep those fixed by \(\sigma\), and map them back. (727/720, 911/960, and as a check the three circles above.)

No rational \(t\) obtained this way is a four-distance point. The ranks were proved from \(L(J,1) \ne 0\) (and \(L(J^{(-1)},1) \ne 0\) where needed) with error bounds, and the torsion of \(E(K)\) was checked in Sage. For 911/960, PARI's ellrank gives only the bounds \([0, 2]\) for \(J\), but \(L(J,1) = 2.737\) with error at most \(1.8 \times 10^{-6}\).

5.2 Finding generators for the Mordell–Weil sieve (four circles)

For the other four circles the Jacobian of pair \(\{1, 2\}\) has root number \(-1\) and rank 1, but ellrank returned no generator. The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer formula predicted generator heights of 80 to 130, out of reach of a direct point search on the curve. We found generators in three ways:

2-coverings. We order the curves isogenous to \(E\) by the constant \(\Omega \prod c_p / |T|^2\) of the BSD formula, largest first, since a larger constant means a smaller expected regulator. On each curve we search the 2-coverings returned by PARI's ell2cover with hyperellratpoints, map a point of infinite order to the curve, and carry it back to \(E\) through an isogeny from ellisomat, followed by saturation.

4-coverings. For 887/960 the 2-covering search found nothing up to height \(7.29 \times 10^6\). The curve has full rational 2-torsion, so the quartic of a 2-covering factors over \(\mathbb{Q}\) as \(g = q_1 q_2\) with quadratics \(q_1, q_2\). A rational point gives \(q_1(x) = d w_1^2\) and \(q_2(x) = d w_2^2\) for a squarefree \(d\) composed of primes dividing \(2\,\mathrm{lc}(q_1)\,\mathrm{lc}(q_2)\,\mathrm{Res}(q_1, q_2)\). For each \(d\) with \(q_1 = d w^2\) solvable we parametrise this conic by \(s\), substitute into \(d\,q_2\), and obtain a quartic \(v^2 = G_d(s)\), a 4-covering. Its coefficients are large (discriminant up to \(10^{62}\)), so we minimise it (hyperellminimalmodel) and reduce it by the Gauss reduction of the positive definite quadratic covariant built from its roots, \(\sum_\theta |x - \theta z|^2 / |G'(\theta)|^{1/2}\). Locally soluble 4-coverings minimise to discriminants near that of the curve, so we search only those within a factor \(10^5\) of the smallest. On 167/480 and 383/984 this reproduces the known generators (heights 79.61 and 86.93) in about one second. On 887/960 it finds the generator at height \(10^6\) on the 4-coverings \(d = -64751\) and \(d = 1638289\).

PARI 2.15.4 and PARI/GP 2.17.2 return different representatives from ell2cover for this curve, and the 4-coverings of the 2.15.4 representative have no point up to height \(10^6\). We therefore computed the generator with 2.17.2 and passed it to the sieve, which checks that it lies on the same minimal model and has infinite order.

With these generators the sieve excludes all four circles, with no control wrongly removed and no cross-check violation. In the second system the \(j\)-invariant, torsion (of order 4) and saturation index 1 agree; mwrank cannot decide the rank of these curves, and rank 1 follows from the \(L'(E,1)\) values in Table 3.

6. What the results rely on

Result 1 rests on the necessary conditions of Section 3.2, whose proofs are in the repository, and on the correctness of our programs. The validation in Sections 3.4 and 3.6 tests the programs; it does not prove them correct.

Result 2 is a computer-assisted proof. Besides our own code (the \(p\)-adic tests, the refined descent, the sieve), it relies on:

  • PARI/GP: ellrank upper bounds for the 588 rank-zero quotients of Section 4.3 and for the sieve curves, torsion, saturation, isogenies, reduction of models.
  • Sage with eclib (second system): \(j\)-invariants, torsion, saturation with the height bound, mwrank, and error-bounded \(L(E,1)\) and \(L'(E,1)\) from lseries().at1 and deriv_at1.
  • The theorems of Gross–Zagier and Kolyvagin, for ranks deduced from \(L\)-values.
One circle with a single-system rank bound.

For \(r = 769/360\) the sieve curve has rank 2. Its upper bound comes only from PARI's ellrank; mwrank could not decide the rank on any curve of the isogeny class, and \(L\)-value methods do not apply to rank 2. Every other rank used in Sections 4.5 and 5 is confirmed by the second system.

The rank-zero exclusions of Section 4.3 (588 circles) use the PARI upper bound without a second system. The descent and the refined descent were tested on circles that carry real points with rational distances to some vertices (372 circles and 2,579 cases); no real point was lost after the correction described in Section 4.4.
